I’ve always been drawn to how unpredictable the stock market feels. One headline drops, and suddenly a company is either flying or collapsing. That tension felt too good to leave as-is, so I turned it into a game.

That’s where Stock Rivals came from.

What Stock Rivals is

At its core, it’s a real-time multiplayer strategy game. Everyone plays as an insider trader—not in the shady sense, but in the sense that you have information others don’t. You’re not just reacting to the market. You’re positioning yourself before it moves.

How the game works

You’re looking at a board with 8 companies. Some are stable, some swing wildly. That difference matters more than you’d think.

Each round, you’re dealt 10 Intel Cards. These cards tell you exactly what’s about to happen to certain stocks. Maybe one jumps +15. Another drops -10. The twist is that prices don’t update immediately.

You get three turns to act before anything changes.

So the whole game becomes a quiet race. You’re buying and selling based on what you know is coming, while everyone else is doing the same with their own hidden info. When the round ends and prices finally shift, that’s when everything snaps into place.

Some stocks show up more often in your hand and move more aggressively. Others are steadier but less rewarding. On top of that, there are special events—things like discounted share offers or ways to recover from a total wipeout—that can completely flip the standings.

Building it without breaking it

I wanted the game to feel fast. Not “it works” fast, but smooth enough that you don’t think about the tech at all.

So the stack was pretty straightforward:

  • React with TypeScript for the frontend
  • Tailwind for a clean, data-heavy UI
  • Socket.IO to keep everything live and in sync
  • Express handling game logic, rooms, and players

Sounds clean on paper. In reality, it got messy.

The problems that actually mattered

1. The mobile problem

Trying to fit a full market dashboard, player actions, and a hand of cards onto a phone screen… that was rough.

The solution wasn’t one big idea. It was a bunch of small decisions—tighter typography, fixed layouts that don’t jump around, and cards that scale just enough to stay readable without taking over the screen.

2. People disconnect. Constantly.

At first, if someone refreshed or lost connection, they were just… gone. Game over.

That wasn’t acceptable.

So I added persistent player IDs using local storage. Now if you drop and come back, the server recognizes you instantly. Your cash, your portfolio, your cards—it’s all still there.

3. Hosting headaches

Early on, the server would randomly go down or freeze mid-game. Not great when you’re in the middle of a tense round.

Memory limits and server sleep cycles were the culprits. Fixing it meant tightening up how the server loads resources and adding a keep-alive system so connections don’t quietly die in the background.

Eventually, I moved everything to a more stable setup. That’s when things finally felt reliable.

Where it landed

Right now, the game runs smoothly, handles reconnects, and keeps up with real-time play without lagging behind.

But more than that, it feels right.

There’s that moment at the end of each round when prices update and you see whether your moves paid off. That’s the whole point. That mix of planning, risk, and surprise.

Building it wasn’t just about getting the mechanics right. It was about making sure the experience holds up—even on a shaky mobile connection, even when someone refreshes at the worst possible time.

Anyway, if you jump in, you’ll get it pretty quickly.
